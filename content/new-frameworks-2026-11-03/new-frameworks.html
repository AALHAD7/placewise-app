Value Chain Analysis

Consulting · Case Structuring · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
Value Chain Analysis is a strategic framework that breaks down a company's operations into sequential primary and support activities to identify cost drivers, profit components, and operational inefficiencies.
example
A supermarket client is facing declining profits despite stable revenue. Using a value chain analysis, you map out costs across stages like raw material procurement, warehousing, store operations, and distribution. You discover that manual inventory tracking in the warehousing stage is causing high spoilage rates and driving up costs.
when_to_use
Use this framework for supply-side, cost-reduction, portfolio analysis, investment, and growth strategy problems.
when_not_to_use
Do not use this framework when the core problem is clearly on the demand or revenue side, such as pricing, customer churn, or falling market share.
steps
  • Map out the company's primary activities (such as inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service) and support activities (such as firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, and procurement).
  • Examine each stage of the value chain sequentially to identify where costs are rising, margins are shrinking, or operational bottlenecks exist.
  • Drill down into specific cost drivers within problematic stages, evaluating factors such as manpower, machinery, methods, and measurements.
  • Synthesize findings to isolate the root cause of inefficiency and develop targeted recommendations for cost reduction or process improvement.
pitfalls
  • Getting bogged down in every minor sub-activity instead of quickly identifying the major cost centers.
  • Treating the value chain as a rigid template rather than customizing the stages to fit the specific industry.
  • Focusing exclusively on costs without considering how operational changes in one link of the chain impact downstream revenue or customer satisfaction.

Dividend Discount Model (Gordon Growth Model)

Finance · Valuation · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The Dividend Discount Model is a financial valuation method that determines the intrinsic value of a company's equity by calculating the present value of its expected future cash dividends.
example
Suppose a company has a Dividend per Share (DPS) of Rs. 10, a Cost of Equity (expected return required by investors) of 12.1%, and a constant dividend growth rate of 5%. Using the Gordon Growth Model formula, the share price is calculated as: $P_0 = \frac{\text{DPS}_1}{\text{k}_e - \text{g}_n} = \frac{10 \times (1 + 5\%)}{12.1\% - 5\%} = \frac{10.50}{0.121 - 0.05} = \text{Rs. } 140.85$.
when_to_use
Use this model for mature companies with predictable financials, nominal expansion needs, and high, stable dividend payout ratios.
when_not_to_use
Do not use this model for start-ups or high-growth companies that pay little to no dividends.
steps
  • Project the company's future earnings down to Earnings per Share (EPS).
  • Determine the Dividend Payout Ratio (the percentage of EPS paid out as dividends) based on historical data and regulatory requirements.
  • Calculate projected future dividends over the investment horizon.
  • Determine the Cost of Equity using the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM, a model relating risk and expected return for equities).
  • Estimate the long-term constant dividend growth rate.
  • Discount the projected dividends and the terminal value back to present value using the Cost of Equity.
pitfalls
  • Relies heavily on projected future numbers rather than hard balance sheet data.
  • Inaccurate when applied to firms with unstable or non-existent dividend payments.
  • Sensitive to small changes in assumptions like the growth rate or Cost of Equity.

DuPont Analysis

Finance · Financial Statements & Accounting · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
DuPont Analysis is a financial framework that breaks down return on equity (ROE) into three distinct drivers—profitability, operating efficiency, and financial leverage—to evaluate a company's underlying performance and identify areas of strength or weakness.
example
A company wants to understand why its return on equity is changing. Using the framework, you break down ROE into three components: Net Profit Margin (Net Income / Sales), Asset Turnover Ratio (Sales / Average Assets), and the Equity Multiplier (Average Assets / Average Equity). If net profit margin is 22.92%, asset turnover is moderate, and leverage is rising, you can pinpoint whether the rate of owner wealth increase is being driven by operational efficiency, asset utilization, or debt.
when_to_use
Use this framework when beginning a financial statement analysis to understand the core drivers behind a company's return on equity.
when_not_to_use
Do not use this framework for non-financial strategic problems like market entry, product mix decisions, or operational bottlenecks.
steps
  • Calculate the net profit margin by dividing net income by sales to measure operational profitability.
  • Calculate the asset turnover ratio by dividing sales by average assets to measure how efficiently assets are used.
  • Calculate the equity multiplier (financial leverage) by dividing average assets by average equity.
  • Multiply the three components together to find the overall return on equity and analyze which factor drives performance.
pitfalls
  • Treating the components in isolation without recognizing that high margins often trade off against low asset turnover.
  • Relying solely on the equity multiplier without assessing the actual risk associated with the company's debt levels.

Treasury Stock Method

Finance · Valuation · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The Treasury Stock Method is an approach used to calculate the dilutive impact of options and warrants on a company's share count by assuming that any cash proceeds received from their exercise are used by the company to repurchase shares in the open market.
example
Assume a company has options to purchase 50,000 shares of common stock at an exercise price of $20 per share, and the average market price of the common stock is $30 per share. Exercising the options generates $1,000,000 in cash ($20 x 50,000). Using these proceeds to buy back shares at the $30 market price repurchases approximately 33,333 shares ($1,000,000 / $30). The net dilution is the difference between the shares created and the shares repurchased: 50,000 - 33,333 = 16,667 new shares added to the diluted share count.
when_to_use
When calculating diluted shares or diluted earnings per share (EPS, a financial metric representing profit allocated to each share of stock) for a company that has outstanding options, warrants, or similar dilutive securities.
when_not_to_use
When evaluating convertible bonds or convertible preferred stock (classes of ownership with preferential rights), because the company does not receive cash proceeds from those conversions.
steps
  • Assume that all in-the-money (options where the strike price is less than the market price) options and warrants are exercised.
  • Determine the total cash proceeds the company receives from the exercise by multiplying the number of options by their exercise price.
  • Calculate how many shares the company can repurchase in the open market by dividing the total cash proceeds by the average market price of the stock.
  • Subtract the number of shares bought back from the number of new shares created upon exercise to find the net incremental shares added to the diluted share count.
pitfalls
  • Using the strike price instead of the average market price when calculating how many shares the company buys back.
  • Applying the method to convertible securities (convertible bonds or preferred stock) where no cash is received.
  • Using the current spot price instead of the average market price for the period when required for EPS calculations.

Precedent Transaction Analysis

Finance · Valuation · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
Precedent Transaction Analysis is a valuation technique used in M&A (mergers and acquisitions) situations to assess the price range that vendors have historically been willing to accept for a business with similar characteristics, based on implied multiples of past transactions.
example
A potential acquiror looking to buy an auto company first identifies a broad set of auto companies, narrows it down to those manufacturing tyres, and finally isolates a tight peer group of tyre-manufacturing auto companies with similar size and business models to establish a relevant valuation benchmark.
when_to_use
When valuing a company or asset during M&A situations, particularly buy-side or sell-side deals, to gauge historical acquisition prices.
when_not_to_use
When performing initial public offerings (IPOs) or add-on financings, where historical transaction multiples are not relevant.
steps
  • Identify Transactions
  • Find Information
  • Calculate Multiples
  • Check and Interpret the Results
pitfalls
  • Using precedent transactions that lack truly similar characteristics or business models
  • Failing to accurately apply and interpret the relevant transaction multiples as benchmarks

Residual Income Model

Finance · Valuation · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The Residual Income Model is a valuation approach that determines a company's equity value by starting with its current book value (common equity) and adding the present value of future residual income, defined as excess returns generated above the cost of capital.
example
To value a bank currently holding $60 million in common equity with a cost of equity of 10% and a projected return on equity (ROE, profitability metric) of 12% yielding $0.8 million in residual income, you discount that residual income back to present value, sum those values over your projection period, and add them directly to the $60 million starting book value.
when_to_use
Use this model when valuing financial institutions like banks, or companies that pay little-to-no dividends where traditional dividend-based models fail.
when_not_to_use
Do not use this model when accounting book values are heavily distorted and fail to reflect the true economic value of the underlying assets.
steps
  • Identify the current book value (common equity) from the balance sheet as your valuation starting point.
  • Calculate annual residual income for each projection year as (ROE - Cost of Equity) multiplied by shareholders' equity.
  • Discount each year's residual income back to present value using the cost of equity (required return for investors).
  • Calculate the terminal value to capture all residual income beyond the explicit projection period.
  • Sum the current book value, the present value of all projected residual incomes, and the present value of the terminal value to arrive at total equity value.
pitfalls
  • Relying on book values that have been distorted by accounting rules or unrecorded intangible assets.
  • Failing to accurately project long-term ROE and cost of equity assumptions for the terminal value calculation.

ADDIE Model

HR · Learning & Development · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The ADDIE Model is a standard instructional design framework used by training designers and experts to build effective training and performance support tools through five distinct phases.
example
Prompt: Design a new digital tools onboarding program for incoming employees. Walkthrough: 1. Analysis: Assess employee skill gaps and learning goals. 2. Design: Outline the curriculum, learning objectives, and delivery methods. 3. Development: Create the actual slide decks, guides, and training videos. 4. Implementation: Roll out the training sessions to the new hires. 5. Evaluation: Gather participant feedback and measure post-training performance to assess effectiveness.
when_to_use
Use when designing and structuring a comprehensive training program or performance support module from scratch.
when_not_to_use
Do not use in fast-paced or highly dynamic environments where a rigid, linear process is too time-consuming and expensive.
steps
  • Analysis
  • Design
  • Development
  • Implementation
  • Evaluation
pitfalls
  • Can be time-consuming and expensive
  • May stifle the creativity of the designer
  • Can be overly linear and inflexible for dynamic work environments

Harvard vs Michigan Models of HRM

HR · HR Strategy · synthesized from 7 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The Harvard vs Michigan Models of HRM are foundational frameworks used to analyze human resource management through either a broad stakeholder-oriented lens (Harvard) or a tight strategic-fit lens aligned with corporate goals (Michigan).
example
Prompt: A legacy newspaper company acquires a digital media firm and faces redundant roles and shifting market environments. Walkthrough: Apply the Harvard vs Michigan Models of HRM by first evaluating how employee policies impact all stakeholders (Harvard) and then ensuring the post-merger HR strategy tightly integrates with the new digital corporate strategy (Michigan).
when_to_use
Use when designing organizational HR strategies, evaluating workforce structures, or aligning human capital management with broader business transformations.
when_not_to_use
Do not use when dealing with narrow, day-to-day administrative payroll tasks that require no strategic evaluation.
steps
  • Identify the core corporate strategy and business environment.
  • Analyze employee interests and broader stakeholder impacts using the Harvard approach.
  • Design a vertically integrated HR strategy that achieves strategic fit with corporate goals using the Michigan approach.
  • Align workforce planning activities, such as recruitment and training, to map current resources to future goals.
pitfalls
  • Treating individual HR processes in silos without integration.
  • Failing to account for changing business environments when designing workforce strategies.

Kirkpatrick Model

HR · Learning & Development · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The Kirkpatrick Model is a widely used four-level framework for evaluating the effectiveness and impact of training programs.
example
A company rolls out a new customer service software. To evaluate it using the Kirkpatrick Model, they would: (1) survey participants immediately to see if they liked the training, (2) test their knowledge of the software via a quiz, (3) observe employees a month later to see if they are actually using the software on the job, and (4) measure whether customer satisfaction scores and resolution times improved across the organization.
when_to_use
Use this framework when designing a comprehensive strategy to measure the success, effectiveness, and organizational impact of a training or development program.
when_not_to_use
Do not use this framework if you need to calculate the explicit financial return on investment (ROI) of a training program, as it stops short of monetary cost-benefit analysis.
steps
  • Level 1: Evaluate Reaction by assessing how favorably participants responded to the training content, facilities, and trainer immediately after completion.
  • Level 2: Evaluate Learning by measuring the extent to which participants improved their knowledge, skills, abilities, and attitudes via tests, simulations, or interviews.
  • Level 3: Evaluate Behavior by determining whether participants successfully transferred what they learned back into their day-to-day workplace performance after some time has passed.
  • Level 4: Evaluate Results by analyzing the broader organizational benefits and outcomes that resulted from the training, such as increased productivity or higher quality.
pitfalls
  • Failing to measure beyond Level 1 (Reaction), which only tells you if employees enjoyed the training rather than if they actually learned anything.
  • Waiting too long to measure Level 2 (Learning) or measuring Level 3 (Behavior) too soon before employees have had adequate time to apply their new skills on the job.

Managerial Grid (Blake-Mouton)

General Management · Leadership & Behavioural · synthesized from 13 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The Managerial Grid (Blake-Mouton) is a multi-phase program and leadership model that maps various leadership styles based on two fundamental orientations: concern for people and concern for production.
example
A company implementing a multi-year development program needs to evaluate and improve its team leadership styles. Step 1: Assess the organization's current leadership styles using the grid's combinations of concern for people and concern for production. Step 2: Progress through the structured improvement phases, moving from individual managerial skills to group dynamics, inter-group relations, corporate planning, implementation, and final evaluation.
when_to_use
When an organization wants to structure a long-term development program to evaluate, train, and improve leadership styles across management.
when_not_to_use
When you need a quick, short-term fix for an immediate operational crisis rather than a multi-year phased training program.
steps
  • Upgrade managerial skills through a structured program
  • Advance to group improvement
  • Improve inter-group relations
  • Engage in corporate planning
  • Develop implementation methods
  • Conclude with an evaluation phase
pitfalls
  • Treating the program as a quick fix instead of a multi-year commitment
  • Focusing too heavily on one orientation (people versus production) at the expense of the other

MoSCoW Method

Prodman · Prioritization · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The MoSCoW method is a prioritization framework used to classify product features or requirements into four distinct buckets based on their importance for a given release.
example
For a new mobile application, the team categorizes a user login feature as Must-Have because the product cannot launch without it, a reset password feature as Should-Have because it is important but not strictly vital for day-one launch, single sign-on as Could-Have because it is a nice-to-have addition, and future advanced analytics as Won't-Have for the current timeframe.
when_to_use
Use this framework when you need to prioritize a list of features or requirements for a specific product release or minimum viable product (MVP, earliest functional version of a product with just enough features to satisfy early customers).
when_not_to_use
Do not use this framework when you need quantitative scoring or deep numerical trade-offs between competing initiatives.
steps
  • Gather all proposed features, user stories (end-user focused descriptions of functionality), or requirements.
  • Evaluate each item against product goals and user needs.
  • Assign each item to one of the four MoSCoW categories: Must-Have, Should-Have, Could-Have, or Won't-Have.
  • Review the distribution with stakeholders (interested parties with a stake in the product) to align on scope and delivery timelines.
pitfalls
  • Overloading the Must-Have bucket with too many features, defeating the purpose of prioritization.
  • Treating Should-Have or Could-Have items as non-negotiable commitments for the immediate launch.

Freemium Model

Prodman · Metrics & Growth · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
The freemium model is a pricing strategy where a company offers core product features for free to attract a large user base while charging for advanced or premium features.
example
A music streaming app offers basic ad-supported listening for free (core free) but charges a monthly fee for offline downloads and higher audio quality (premium features paid).
when_to_use
Use when evaluating growth strategies for high-volume consumer products or digital services that benefit from network effects.
when_not_to_use
Do not use when serving niche enterprise clients with extremely high per-user serving costs and no consumer network effects.
steps
  • Define the free tier scope
  • Identify premium features
  • Analyze conversion rates
  • Optimize monetization channels
pitfalls
  • Giving away too much value for free
  • Failing to convert free users to paid tiers
  • Underestimating the server or support costs of serving free users

Net Promoter Score

Marketing:Sales · Customer & Brand · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
Net Promoter Score is a single metric ranging from -100 to 100 that measures customer loyalty and the likelihood that customers will recommend a product or service to others.
example
A food delivery service surveys its customers and asks: 'Would you recommend this service to a friend?' on a scale of 0 to 10. If 60% of respondents give a 9 or 10 (promoters) and 20% give a 0 through 6 (detractors), the Net Promoter Score is calculated as 60% minus 20%, resulting in a score of 40.
when_to_use
Use it to track high-level trends in customer sentiment and brand advocacy over time.
when_not_to_use
Do not use it as a standalone measure to diagnose root causes of operational problems or to compare vastly different products and markets.
steps
  • Survey customers using a single question asking how likely they are to recommend the product or service on a scale of 0 to 10.
  • Categorize respondents giving 9 or 10 as promoters.
  • Categorize respondents giving 7 or 8 as neutral or lukewarm.
  • Categorize respondents giving 0 through 6 as detractors.
  • Calculate the final score by subtracting the percentage of detractors from the percentage of promoters.
pitfalls
  • Overlooking specific regional or demographic outliers by relying solely on the aggregate score.
  • Ignoring the underlying product, delivery, or customer service details that caused low ratings.
  • Assuming that the same numerical score holds identical meaning across different cultures, industries, or product types.

Customer Satisfaction Score (CSAT)

Marketing:Sales · Customer & Brand · synthesized from 14 casebook excerpt(s) across multiple source files
description
Customer Satisfaction Score (CSAT) is a metric typically measured on a scale (such as 1 to 5) that quantifies how happy customers are with a company's product, service, or specific touchpoints.
example
A pizza company notices a recent dip in its delivery CSAT. Breaking the customer journey down into pre-ordering, ordering-to-delivery, and post-ordering reveals that increased delivery distance and slower speed are driving down the score.
when_to_use
Use when a client is experiencing a decline in customer satisfaction or retention and needs to diagnose operational bottlenecks across the customer journey.
when_not_to_use
Do not use when the primary problem is top-of-funnel brand awareness or broad financial restructuring unrelated to customer experience.
steps
  • Identify which segment, product line, or channel (such as delivery vs. dine-in) is experiencing a change in satisfaction scores.
  • Map out the end-to-end customer journey (such as pre-ordering, ordering-to-delivery, and post-delivery).
  • Isolate specific operational pain points within each phase of the journey (such as packaging issues, wait times, or app UI/UX).
  • Analyze internal factors (such as logistics, call center wait times, or staff efficiency) versus external factors (such as geography or competitors).
  • Formulate targeted short-term fixes and long-term strategic recommendations to resolve the root causes.
pitfalls
  • Failing to segment the data to find localized or demographic outlier scores.
  • Focusing on the overall score without breaking down the specific steps of the customer journey where the drop actually occurred.
  • Neglecting to check whether competitors are facing similar satisfaction dips due to broader industry trends.
